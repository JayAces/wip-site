How This Started

Jason didn't build WIP from a study. He built it from 41 years of living with sickle cell disease.

Every morning starts the same way it always has. Before anything else, he checks where his body is. Not what the day requires of him. Where he actually is. Hospitals spent his childhood telling him to expect less, so for a long time that was how he lived: in bursts, never quite planning past the next flare.

That changed when he started actually listening to what his body was telling him instead of just pushing through it. He built the earliest version of WIP for himself, tracking his own crises, his own triggers, his own patterns, because the tool he needed didn't exist yet. He is patient zero. It worked on him before it ever reached anyone else.

Ten days after he opened it up to the community, a Warrior logged something that changed how he understood what he'd built. Buried in her submission was a pattern no clinical paper had ever documented: her crises tracked to her menstrual cycle, a trigger standard ER intake never asks about. Not a clinician found it. Not a researcher. A Warrior, using the same tool Jason built for himself, saw her own pattern the way he'd learned to see his.

That confirmed what he'd already started to suspect. The most important clinical intelligence about sickle cell disease was trapped inside the people living it, and the system built to help them was, at best, ignoring that intelligence. At worst, and this is the part that matters, with 32% of ER visits ending in protocol violations and clinicians citing addiction risk instead of listening, it was weaponizing stigma against the very people it was supposed to serve.

So WIP grew into something bigger than one Warrior's morning ritual: a tracker that treats every Warrior's experience as the evidence it actually is. No accounts. No medical records. No gatekeepers. Three minutes. Anonymous. Yours.

145 crisis submissions from 113 Warriors across 28 states have now come in. The data compounds. The patterns surface. And for the first time, when a Warrior walks into an ER, they carry a documented trajectory, not just a moment of pain.

Value One

Your Body Is the First Instrument

Declaration

We believe that Warriors know their bodies before any chart, any clinician, or any algorithm does. Your lived experience is not anecdotal. It is intelligence. The system that treats it otherwise is the one that's broken.

This is the foundational value. It rejects the hierarchy that places clinical observation above lived knowledge. The menstrual cycle trigger discovery, surfaced by Warriors and not researchers, is the proof. When the community submitted their data, they told us something the clinical literature had missed entirely. That is not coincidence. That is expertise the system chose not to collect.

Value Two

Anonymous Power Over Institutional Permission

Declaration

We believe your data should protect you, not expose you. No accounts. No medical records. No gatekeepers. Your Warrior ID belongs to you, not a hospital, not an insurer, not a system that's already failed you.

This drives the Tally form design: anonymous by default, Warrior ID self-created, never linked to identity. Power without surveillance. In a system that has historically used a Warrior's own health data against them, to deny claims, to question legitimacy, to cite addiction, anonymity isn't a privacy preference. It's a structural protection.

III
Value Three

Patterns Over Episodes

Declaration

We believe a crisis is not a moment. It is a pattern. The system treats you like you showed up out of nowhere. We build the record they won't: your trajectory, your triggers, your evidence. When you walk into an ER, you carry documented intelligence, not just a moment of pain.

This maps directly to the three-step process: Log Your Crisis → Build Your Record → Shape the System. It reframes the 23-hour gap between crisis onset and hospital care as a design flaw in the system, not a failure of the Warrior. Every submission builds longitudinal evidence. Over time, that evidence becomes undeniable, a documented trajectory that changes what happens in the ER encounter.

The Gap 23 hrs

You get about an hour with the clinician. You answer their questions and recall as best you can, in pain, from memory. The other twenty-three hours of the day, nobody is watching and nothing is recorded. That is the gap. WIP is what fills it.

6.1 / 10

Average reported pain level at the moment of logging, median 7, across 145 submissions. Warriors are not logging acute moments. They are logging sustained, ongoing trajectories the ER has never seen.

V
Value Five

The 23-Hour Gap Is Not Silence. It's a Choice They Made

Declaration

We believe the gap between crisis onset and hospital care is not natural. It is engineered by a system that profits from your invisibility. We exist to close it, with data, with patterns, with the truth that Warriors already carry.

This is the combative value, the one that names the enemy without naming a person. The "23-Hour Gap" is not an accident of biology or logistics. It is a consequence of a system that was designed without Warriors' input, that treats their crises as inconveniences rather than medical emergencies, and that has built its protocols around the absence of the very data WIP is now collecting. Negative State + Positive Adaptability = Growth. That equation is ours.
